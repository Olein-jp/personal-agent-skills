# Business-Site Article Finalization

## Purpose and Scope

Use this reference for one limited finishing pass before returning an Olein Design business-site blog article. It is not a full rewrite, an alternate draft, or a substitute for publication QA.

Follow the user's explicit instructions, Article Brief, Task Packet, and medium- or project-specific guidance before this reference. Do not invent missing services, service scope, achievements, contact details, URLs, CTAs, or author and client experiences. Apply only the checks that matter to the article's actual purpose and supplied materials.

## A. Scope Boundary

- Check that the article makes clear what the reader can reasonably decide from it and what still requires separate confirmation.
- Do not settle questions outside the article's researched scope, such as a particular service's specifications or data handling, contract terms, or legal, medical, or accounting judgments.
- When the boundary affects the reader's next decision, state briefly what the article covers and what must be checked elsewhere.
- Do not add external facts during this finishing pass when the Article Brief did not require them.

## B. Conditional Recommendations

- Review claims equivalent to `can`, `suitable`, `easy`, `safe`, or `recommended`.
- When usefulness depends on the available input, comparison method, consequence of an error, permissions, or operating process, place the decision-changing condition close to the claim.
- Keep only material conditions. Do not attach a disclaimer to every sentence.
- Do not guarantee safety merely because a person reviews the result.

## C. Capability and Responsibility Claims

- Review broad statements such as `AI cannot ...` or `the tool decides ...` that generalize a product's capability or responsibility beyond what the article establishes.
- Prefer stating what must not be finalized from a particular output alone within the described workflow, rather than making an essential claim about the product.
- Make clear who is responsible for confirmation, approval, publication or execution, and explaining the reason for a decision when those roles matter.
- Preserve a definite operational meaning; do not make a claim vague merely to soften it.

For example, use the distinction below as a reasoning aid, not as text to paste mechanically into an article:

```text
Too broad:
AI cannot make a responsible decision.

Prefer:
Do not use the AI output alone to finalize information that has not yet been
approved inside the organization.
```

## D. Reader-Stage Internal Links

- When several related articles are available, route readers according to what remains unresolved at their current stage instead of packing every link into one paragraph.
- Briefly explain what the reader should clarify in each linked article before continuing with the current one.
- Do not repeat the linked article's full explanation in the current article.
- Do not invent related articles, titles, or final URLs when they have not been supplied or verified.

## E. Example Completeness

- For a hypothetical example or procedural example, check which stages the conclusion actually needs: input, work, confirmation, approval, execution or publication, and post-execution confirmation.
- Include post-publication or post-send confirmation only when the resulting state matters to the article. Do not force it into every example.
- Never present a hypothetical example as the author's, Olein Design's, or a real client's experience.

## F. Self-Service and Consultation Boundary

- Describe concrete conditions under which readers can check or proceed on their own.
- Describe conditions that warrant outside consultation without using fear or anxiety as persuasion.
- When the issue belongs to another specialty, make clear that the appropriate adviser may differ.
- Include information worth preparing before consultation only when the project guidance or Article Brief calls for it.
- Do not force fixed headings such as "what you can do yourself" and "when to consult" when the distinction is clearer in the surrounding prose or another structure.

## G. CTA Consistency

- Check that the CTA follows from the article's central conclusion and decision criteria.
- If the article recognizes not using a tool, using it for only part of the work, or relying on an existing feature, keep those as legitimate options in the CTA rather than recommending only adoption of a specific tool.
- Confirm the available service scope from supplied project materials. Do not invent an unverified service or contact route.
- If a CTA is required but its service details or URL are missing, complete the useful body without inventing them; identify the missing information or use a clearly labeled placeholder limited to the requested CTA.
- Do not exaggerate risk or urgency to obtain an inquiry.
- Connect the CTA naturally to the difficulty discussed in the article instead of pasting the same boilerplate every time.

## H. Compression and Structure

- Check whether the central concept can be expressed as a short contrast or a one-sentence key message.
- Consider a table when at least three items share meaningful comparison dimensions.
- Consider numbered headings or a numbered list when readers will need to find steps, decision criteria, or stages again later.
- Use short prose when it is clearer; do not make tables or numbering a goal in themselves.
- Do not increase the amount of information during this pass. Compress duplicate explanations and preserve only what supports the reader's decision.

## One-Pass Revision Rule

Revise only the passages where these checks reveal a material problem. Do not rewrite unaffected paragraphs, generate a second outline, or produce an alternate article. After the targeted revisions, confirm that the meaning, factual accuracy, Olein voice, beginner-friendly explanations, and Japanese notation remain intact, then continue to the final self-check in `SKILL.md`.
